\hspace{0.75cm}{\bf {\Large {~~~~~~~~~~~~Response to Comments of Reviewer 1}}}

\noindent
\textbf{Comment 1:} \textit{The paper aims to demonstrate convergence compatibility with FedTSKD and client selection. However, the convergence proof is based on FedTSKD, and the effect of client selection via a client subset with bounded variance can easily be derived. Therefore, the analytical contribution of Section V-B is minimal.} \\
\noindent
\textbf{Reply 1:} {We thank the Reviewer for this comment and we accept it. The old Section~V-B stated that the per-client bound of [13] continues to hold under our selector. That statement is close to a tautology and the Reviewer is right that it can be obtained with no effort. The reason is that [13] contains no client-selection mechanism at all. Its Algorithm~1 admits every client in every round, its Section~VI states that full participation is adopted by design, and its proof of Theorem~1 tracks a single client through its own weight sequence without reading the state of any other client. A bound that was never conditioned on a selection rule cannot be disturbed by one.

We have rewritten the section around the question that does carry content. The index $t$ in the bound of [13] counts the local-training and distillation steps a client has actually taken. Under full participation that index moves with the round counter, because every client passes through every phase of every round. Under partial participation it does not, because a client's clock advances only in the rounds where it is selected. The bound of [13] therefore says nothing about the number of communication rounds, which is the budget an operator actually pays for. Closing that gap needs a lower bound on how often each client is served, and that is what our Proposition~1 supplies.

The revised Section~V-B now gives two corollaries. Corollary~1 composes the unmodified bound of [13] with Proposition~1 and gives $\mathcal{O}(N/(KR))$ in communication rounds for every client. Corollary~2 carries the same rate to the global objective. Both recover the $\mathcal{O}(1/R)$ rate of [13] at $K=N$, which is the consistency check we would expect since a selector has nothing left to choose once the cohort is the whole pool.

We also state plainly what these corollaries do not give. They do not beat uniform random selection in order, since both are $\mathcal{O}(N/(KR))$. What the deterministic rotation adds is that the bound holds for each client at every finite $R$ rather than only on average. A policy that balances participation in expectation cannot make that statement, because a single realisation may leave a client well below $KR/N$. This is the same distinction Proposition~1 already draws for the participation counts, now carried through to the loss. We have not claimed more than that. The revised material is in Section~V-B of the manuscript.}\\

\textbf{Comment 2:} \textit{Only two datasets, Fashion-MNIST and MNIST, which have very similar characteristics, are tested. The dataset is compatible with the given model. However, as mentioned in the conclusion and future work, the applicability of the proposed method to domains other than images is questionable, as the proposed SCOPE-FD was tested under a narrow scenario.} \\
\noindent
\textbf{Reply 2:} {We thank the Reviewer for this comment. We accept that two grayscale $28 \times 28$ benchmarks were too narrow a basis for the claim, and we have addressed the point directly by evaluating the selector on a domain outside images.

We ran the selector on the Free Spoken Digit Dataset, a ten-class corpus of spoken digits represented as log-spectrograms and paired with an \texttt{AudioCNN} model. Over five seeds the proposed selector reaches $46.16 \pm 1.69$, the debt-only variant $45.54 \pm 1.17$, uniform random $44.99 \pm 1.59$, SubTrunc $40.66 \pm 1.48$ and DivFL $40.53 \pm 1.49$. The participation Gini is $1.33\%$ against $12.49\%$ for uniform random and about $45\%$ for the two submodular methods. The proposed selector is ahead of uniform random on every one of the five seeds, by between $0.51$ and $1.70$ percentage points, and ahead of both submodular methods on every seed by at least four percentage points.

We also report the limitation of this corpus plainly. It holds roughly ninety training samples per client, which is about twenty times less than Fashion-MNIST, so the absolute accuracy is low and the ordering of the selectors rather than the level is the informative quantity. Two settings were adjusted for the same reason, namely three local epochs in place of one and a public set of one hundred and fifty samples, and both adjustments are stated in the manuscript.

We also completed the MNIST study over three seeds, where the debt-only variant reaches $90.88 \pm 0.42$, uniform random $90.58 \pm 0.43$ and the complete score $90.50 \pm 0.56$, with DivFL at $88.48 \pm 0.43$ and SubTrunc at $87.91 \pm 0.50$.

The reading across the three datasets is consistent. The fairness separation is identical everywhere, because it is determined by the participation arithmetic alone. The accuracy ordering is also preserved, with the proposed selector and the debt-only variant tied with each other and ahead of the two submodular methods. What does change across datasets is the absolute accuracy level, which reflects the difficulty of the task rather than the selection rule.

In the same revision we removed the earlier EMNIST replication, which used a single seed and was the last remaining single-seed result in the paper. The multi-seed audio and MNIST studies supersede it and address the Reviewer's concern more directly, since one of them leaves the image domain entirely. These studies are in Section~VI-M of the revised manuscript.}\\

\noindent
\textbf{Reply 6:} {We thank the Reviewer for raising both points. We have addressed each one with new experiments.

For the heterogeneity, we now sweep the Dirichlet parameter over $\alpha \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 5.0\}$ and we also include an independent and identically distributed setting. The behaviour is consistent across the whole range. At $\alpha = 0.05$, which is severe heterogeneity, SCOPE-FD reaches $26.74 \pm 4.19$ and random selection reaches $27.07 \pm 4.24$. At $\alpha = 5.0$, which is mild heterogeneity, SCOPE-FD reaches $84.67 \pm 0.11$ and random selection reaches $84.60 \pm 0.18$. Accuracy is therefore a tie at every level of heterogeneity. The participation Gini is $1.33\%$ for SCOPE-FD and $12.49\%$ for random selection at every level. The fairness advantage is therefore invariant to the degree of heterogeneity.

For the scale, we now report client pools of $N \in \{47, 50, 53, 100, 200\}$ at participation ratios of five, ten, and twenty percent. The fairness result strengthens as the pool grows. At $N = 200$ with $K = 10$, SCOPE-FD reaches $51.27\%$ with a Gini of $0.00\%$, while random selection reaches $48.84\%$ with a Gini of $24.16\%$.

We also report the cases where SCOPE-FD does not lead, and the scale study is now large enough to explain them rather than merely list them. Fifteen configurations pair SCOPE-FD against random selection on matched seeds, spanning $N$ from $30$ to $200$ and participation ratios from $3.3$ to $33$ percent. The accuracy gap falls monotonically as the ratio grows, with a Spearman rank correlation of $-0.815$ at $p = 0.0002$. The debt-only gap over random selection is positive in all fifteen configurations, from $+0.05$ to $+2.39$ percentage points. The three configurations where the complete score trails all use a cohort of at least twenty clients, and in all three the debt-only variant leads the complete score. Balanced rotation therefore does not cost accuracy at any ratio we tested. The two information terms are what turn the gap negative once the cohort is large, which is consistent with the mechanism, since a cohort of twenty or forty clients already covers most of the label space. We state in the revised manuscript that the accuracy claim is confined to the sparse regime and that a deployment at a high participation ratio should prefer the debt-only variant. We also note that several of these gaps are a small fraction of the seed-to-seed spread, so the claim concerns the direction of the effect rather than its magnitude. The heterogeneity sweep is in Section~VI-E and Fig.~3. The scaling study is in Section~VI-L and Fig.~6, and the new ratio analysis is collected in Fig.~7 of the revised manuscript.}\\

\textbf{Reply 3:} {We thank the Reviewer for this comment. We have taken the first of the two options offered, and we did not need to fall back on the second.

The assumptions of [13] are smoothness and strong convexity of the local training and distillation losses and bounded variance and bounded norm of their stochastic gradients. None of the four refers to the selected subset. The phrase ``uniformly and independently'' in its Assumption~3 describes the minibatch drawn inside a client rather than the way clients are drawn. Its Algorithm~1 admits all clients in every round and its Section~VI adopts full participation by design, so the analysis in [13] has no client-sampling step for a deterministic rotation to reconcile with. In [13] the index $t$ counts the steps a client has actually taken, and under full participation that coincides with the round counter. Under our selector it does not, since a client's clock advances only in the rounds where it is selected, so the bound of [13] on its own says nothing in terms of communication rounds.

We do not claim a rate that improves on uniform random selection in order. Both are $\mathcal{O}(N/(KR))$. The gain is that our bound holds for each client at every finite horizon rather than in expectation. We state this limitation in Section~V-B and again in the Conclusion. Please refer to Section~V-B of the revised manuscript.}\\

\noindent
\textbf{Comment 1:} \textit{The authors clearly explain why traditional federated learning client selection methods cannot be directly applied to federated distillation.} \\
\noindent
\textbf{Reply 1:} {We thank the Reviewer for this request. We agree that this argument was asserted in prose and not demonstrated. We now demonstrate it. We took Oort, which is a standard utility-based selector from federated learning, and ran it unmodified inside the federated distillation pipeline at the headline configuration. The result is decisive. Oort reaches $21.14 \pm 0.60$ accuracy with a participation Gini of $83.33\%$. SCOPE-FD reaches $71.21 \pm 0.99$ with a Gini of $1.33\%$. Random selection reaches $70.99 \pm 1.38$. The gap is over fifty percentage points.

The reason is the aggregation rule. In federated distillation the server averages logits on the public set with weights proportional to client data size. A utility-based selector repeatedly picks the same small group of high-loss clients. In federated learning this is useful because those updates are averaged into the global model. In federated distillation it instead narrows the set of clients that contribute to the consensus logits, so the distillation target collapses toward a small and biased subset. High per-client utility does not translate into a better consensus target. This experiment gives the direct evidence for the argument that previously appeared only as text. The port is described in Section~II-A and the measurement is in Section~VI-C and Table~III of the revised manuscript.}\\

\textbf{Comment 1 (Convergence analysis):} \textit{The manuscript states that the convergence behavior of FedTSKD is preserved because SCOPE-FD only changes the composition of the selected client subset while leaving the remaining learning procedure unchanged. Since deterministic partial participation introduces a different client-selection policy, it would be helpful to clarify whether the assumptions required in the original convergence analysis of FedTSKD remain satisfied. If a complete theoretical extension is beyond the scope of this work, a brief discussion of the underlying assumptions would improve the presentation.} \\
\noindent
\textbf{Reply 1:} {We thank the Reviewer for this comment. It asks the right question, and answering it changed how we present the section. Section~V-B now works through the assumptions one at a time.

Assumptions~1 and~2 of [13] are smoothness and strong convexity of the local training loss and the distillation loss. They are properties of those loss functions alone, and our selector changes no loss function, so they are untouched.

Assumptions~3 and~4 of [13] are bounded variance and bounded gradient norm under within-client minibatch sampling. The phrase ``uniformly and independently'' in Assumption~3 describes the minibatch drawn inside a client's own dataset. It does not describe how clients are drawn. Our selector never touches minibatch sampling, so these two are untouched as well.

The aggregation step reads the selected subset only through the data-size-weighted logit averaging rule, which we use exactly as [13] defines it.

The most direct answer is the fourth one. There is no client-sampling assumption in [13] for a deterministic rotation either to satisfy or to violate. Its Algorithm~1 admits all $N$ clients in every round, its Section~VI states that full participation is adopted by design, and its proof of Theorem~1 tracks one client through its own weight sequence without reading the state of any other client. The absence of a selection mechanism in the algorithm is why no step of the proof depends on one.

We went further than the brief discussion the Reviewer offers as a sufficient response, because the assumption audit showed that a full extension was within reach. What partial participation genuinely changes is not an assumption but the time index. The index $t$ in [13] counts the steps a client has actually taken, which coincides with the round counter only under full participation. Our Proposition~1 lower-bounds how often each client is served, and composing the two gives Corollary~1 and Corollary~2 in Section~V-B, with a rate of $\mathcal{O}(N/(KR))$ in communication rounds that holds for every client and returns the rate of [13] at $K=N$. Please refer to Section~V-B of the revised manuscript.}\\

\textbf{Comment 2 (Fairness evaluation):} \textit{The theoretical participation-fairness guarantee is one of the main strengths of the manuscript. However, the experimental validation could be expanded to demonstrate the proposed behavior under a broader range of settings. For example, additional experiments using different values of N and R, configurations where K does not divide N, or evaluation windows that do not exactly coincide with complete participation cycles would provide stronger empirical evidence for the generality of the proposed fairness behavior.} \\
\noindent
\textbf{Reply 2:} {We are grateful to the Reviewer for this suggestion. It asks for exactly the three stress tests that the original fairness evidence lacked, and we have added all three.

For different client-pool sizes, the revised paper covers $N \in \{30, 47, 50, 53, 100, 200\}$ at several participation ratios.

For the case where $K$ does not divide $N$, we added dedicated configurations. At $N = 47$ with $K = 6$, SCOPE-FD reaches a Gini of $1.40\%$ while random selection reaches $15.70\%$. At $N = 53$ with $K = 7$, SCOPE-FD reaches $1.25\%$ while random selection reaches $13.81\%$. The guarantee therefore degrades gracefully rather than breaking. The small residual arises because the total number of selections is not an exact multiple of the pool size, so the counts cannot all be equal.

For evaluation windows that do not align with a participation cycle, we added a rolling-window Gini. It is computed over a window that is deliberately not aligned with the cycle length. SCOPE-FD stays between $4.52\%$ and $18.58\%$ across all tested configurations, while random selection stays between $43.85\%$ and $52.22\%$. The advantage therefore does not depend on measuring at a cycle boundary.

For different numbers of rounds, we note that every run records the full per-round history, so the fairness metrics can be read at any horizon. We now report the fairness metrics at $R \in \{25, 50, 75, 100\}$ rather than only at the final round.

Working through these three tests led us to a result we had missed, and we thank the Reviewer for prompting it. The proof of Proposition~1 bounds the Gini coefficient by replacing an exact numerator with its maximum. Keeping that numerator gives the cumulative participation Gini in closed form,
\[
G^{(R)} = \frac{m\,(N-m)}{N K R}, \qquad m = KR \bmod N ,
\]
which requires no new assumption and follows from the same induction. This single expression answers all three parts of the Reviewer's comment at once. It gives the value at any $N$, at any $R$, and whether or not $K$ divides $N$.

It also corrects a statement we had made carelessly. We had written that the coefficient is zero wherever $K$ divides $N$. The correct condition is that $N$ divides $KR$, which involves the horizon as well as the pool and the cohort. The two conditions are not equivalent, and our own data contains counterexamples in both directions. At the headline setting $N=30$, $K=5$, $R=100$ the cohort divides the pool, yet the coefficient is $1.33\%$ because $KR$ leaves a remainder of $20$. At $N=50$ with $K=3$ the cohort does not divide the pool, yet the coefficient is exactly zero because $KR = 300$ is a multiple of $50$. We have corrected the statement and the caption of Fig.~6 accordingly.

We then tested the expression against the whole campaign rather than against selected configurations. It predicts the measured value correctly in $457$ of $457$ selector runs, with a largest deviation below $10^{-4}$ percentage points, and it holds across every dataset, heterogeneity level, SNR, privacy mechanism, staleness window and public-set variation we ran. The only runs it does not cover are the eighteen dropout runs, where a selected client may fail to return, so realised participation differs from intended participation. We regard this as a considerably stronger answer than the three separate stress tests we had prepared, and it exists because the Reviewer asked for the general case. The closed form and its consequences are in Section~V-A, and the campaign-wide test is in Section~VI-N of the revised manuscript.}\\

\textbf{Comment 5 (Privacy discussion):} \textit{The proposed method requires each client to upload its normalized label histogram. \noindent
\textbf{Reply 5:} {We thank the Reviewer for this comment, which matches the second comment of Reviewer 2. We have replaced the brief discussion with a measured study and an explicit statement of what is disclosed.

What the server learns is the normalized label histogram of each client, which is a $C$-dimensional vector of class proportions. It does not learn any sample, any label of any sample, or the size of the local dataset. The disclosure is static, since the histogram is uploaded once rather than per round, so it does not leak the evolution of the local data.

We then measured what it costs to remove that disclosure. The first remedy applies a Laplace mechanism to the histogram before the selector reads it, swept over $\varepsilon \in \{0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 5\}$. The second removes the disclosure entirely by replacing the histogram with a server-side surrogate formed from the public-set logits each client already submits. Against $71.99 \pm 0.63$ for the undefended selector, the Laplace variants reach $72.01 \pm 0.86$ at $\varepsilon = 0.1$, $71.71 \pm 0.72$ at $\varepsilon = 0.5$, $71.97 \pm 0.69$ at $\varepsilon = 1$, $71.78 \pm 0.80$ at $\varepsilon = 2$ and $71.85 \pm 0.73$ at $\varepsilon = 5$. The practical implication is that the histogram is optional rather than central. The histogram feeds only the under-prediction bonus and the class-coverage penalty, and our four-way ablation shows that those two terms do not change final accuracy. The participation-debt term that carries the fairness guarantee never reads the histogram at all. A deployment that regards the disclosure as unacceptable can therefore adopt the surrogate, or the debt-only variant, and lose nothing measurable.
